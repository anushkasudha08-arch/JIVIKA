How to read the results

What the app can and cannot know

  • Distance is a straight line, not road distance. Hills, rivers and traffic can make it longer.
  • A big hospital may be farther but can treat more — read which level of care you need.
  • Phone numbers appear only when someone mapped them. Call 104 if you cannot reach a facility.
  • Government-tagged means the map's ownership tag says government or public. It is not proof of services, cost or scheme eligibility.
  • Laboratories come from the map's laboratory tag. Test availability and prices are never shown — call first.
  • This list is not a recommendation or a ranking of quality.

Before you travel

Small steps that save a wasted trip

  • Call ahead and ask if the doctor or gynaecologist is available today.
  • Carry your ID, any previous prescriptions, reports and medicine strips.
  • Note the first day of your last period and your symptoms.
  • Take a family member or ASHA worker if you feel weak or dizzy.
  • For a government hospital, ask about the outpatient (OPD) timings before leaving.

If you cannot travel

Care by phone and video

Call 104 to speak to a health worker in your language. Ask your ASHA worker about home visits, or use eSanjeevani for a free government video consultation. A phone consultation cannot examine you — if symptoms are severe, go in person or call 108.

Where to go

The public health system, step by step

Start at the lowest level that can handle your problem. Going straight to a district hospital for a small problem means long queues; staying home for a serious one is dangerous.

1

ASHA worker

Your first contact in the village

A trained woman from your own village. She can check your blood pressure, give iron and folic acid tablets, arrange a pregnancy check-up, and tell you where to go.

  • Pregnancy and newborn care, immunisation
  • Family planning and menstrual problems
  • Referral and transport to a facility

Free. Usually available every day in the village.

2

Sub-centre / ANM

Basic care and tests

Run by an Auxiliary Nurse Midwife (ANM), often for 3–5 villages. Good for a first check, iron tablets, immunisation and simple medicines.

  • Fever, cough, body pain and minor injuries
  • Antenatal checks and haemoglobin testing
  • Contraception and health advice

Free or very low cost. Morning hours on fixed days.

3

PHC / Arogya Mandir

Doctor and basic lab

A primary health centre has a doctor, nurses and a small laboratory. Most common women's health problems can be treated here.

  • Period problems, infections, anaemia
  • Diabetes and blood pressure checks
  • General medicines from the pharmacy

Free for most services. Ask for the OPD day and timing.

4

CHC

Specialists for several PHCs

A community health centre is usually a 30-bed hospital with a surgeon, physician, gynaecologist and paediatrician, plus X-ray and a laboratory.

  • Pregnancy complications and delivery
  • Severe period pain and heavy bleeding
  • Minor surgery, fractures and blood tests

Free or low cost. Ask which days the gynaecologist sits.

5

District hospital

Full specialist care

Hundreds of beds, a blood bank, ultrasound, CT or MRI, intensive care and operation theatres. This is where most serious women's health problems are treated.

  • Heavy bleeding, severe pain, injury
  • Cancer checks, biopsy and blood transfusion
  • Second opinion when treatment has not worked

Free or low cost. Take a referral if you have one.

6

Medical college

Rare and complex problems

For cancer care, complex surgery, IVF or conditions that need many specialists together. Usually in the district headquarters.

  • Cancer diagnosis and treatment
  • Complex pregnancy and newborn intensive care
  • Second opinion after treatment has not worked

Ask about the free scheme counter and referral requirement.

When in doubt, call 104 and describe your symptoms. The health worker will tell you which level to go to.

A specialist for women

How to find a good gynaecologist

A gynaecologist is a doctor trained in women's reproductive health. You do not need a referral to see one at most government hospitals.

  1. 1
    Ask at your PHC first. A medical officer can treat most problems and refer you to the gynaecologist at the CHC or district hospital if needed.
  2. 2
    Look for the OBG department. In government hospitals it is called Obstetrics and Gynaecology (OBG) and is listed on the hospital's OPD board.
  3. 3
    Check registration in private clinics. You can ask to see the doctor's registration number and the clinic's board. Do not use unregistered “ladies' clinics”.
  4. 4
    For a second opinion, carry your records. Reports, prescriptions and scan films help the new doctor understand what has already been tried.

Questions worth asking

You are allowed to ask all of these

  • What is the likely cause of my symptoms?
  • Which tests do I need, and where can I get them free?
  • What are the side effects of this medicine?
  • Do I need to come back, and when?
  • What warning signs should bring me back sooner?

Carry these to the visit

It saves a second trip

  • Any government ID and your health card or ABHA number, if you have one.
  • All medicines you take, or their strips and prescriptions.
  • Dates of your last two or three periods.
  • Previous scans, blood reports and discharge papers.
Affordable care

Free and low-cost options

You do not have to pay private fees for basic care. These services exist in most districts.

Jan Aushadhi pharmacy

Generic medicines at low prices

Government-run shops sell quality generic medicines, often 50–90% cheaper than branded versions. Ask the pharmacist to explain how to take them — the medicine is the same, only the name differs.

Someone to call

Health and support helplines

All of these are free to call from any phone in India. You can ask in your own language.

What to say when you call: give your name, age, village or town, the main symptom, when it started, and whether you are pregnant. If you have a phone number for a facility, save it before an emergency.

Need help deciding where to go?

Describe your symptoms to the health assistant. It can explain which level of care fits, without diagnosing you.
