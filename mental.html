Does your mood follow your cycle?

PMS is common — severe PMS is treatable

In the week before a period, many women notice irritability, tearfulness, bloating or trouble sleeping. That is PMS. When it is severe enough to disrupt work or relationships every month, it may be PMDD — and it deserves proper medical care, not being told to “adjust”.

  • Log mood daily for two cycles so the pattern becomes visible.
  • Steady sleep, movement and regular meals reduce symptoms.
  • Show the record to a doctor if it affects your life every month.

You are not alone

Mood changes are normal. They are not a character flaw, a weakness, or something you have to hide. Many women feel low or anxious around their periods, after childbirth, during menopause, or when money and family pressures pile up.

Talking about it — to a friend, an ASHA worker, a counsellor or a doctor — is a sign of strength, not failure.

When to talk to a professional

These are not signs of weakness

  • Low mood, worry or hopelessness almost every day for more than 2 weeks
  • Sleep or appetite has changed a lot, or you cannot manage work or daily tasks
  • Panic attacks, or fear that stops you leaving the house
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life — get help today
  • After childbirth: constant sadness, guilt, or difficulty bonding with the baby
  • After violence, abuse or a miscarriage — support is available and it matters

Only a qualified professional can diagnose a mental health condition. A website, including this one, cannot.

        Ask for help

        People you can call

        Free, confidential and available in many languages. You do not need a diagnosis or a reason that sounds serious enough.

        What to say when you call

        You can read these lines aloud

        • “I have felt low or worried almost every day for more than two weeks.”
        • “I am not sleeping or eating properly, and I cannot cope with daily work.”
        • “I have thoughts that frighten me and I need to talk to someone.”
        • “I am safe right now, but I need help to stay safe.”

        It is okay to cry, to pause, or to say you do not know where to start. The person on the line is trained for this.

        If you feel unsafe right now

        A simple safety plan

        1. 1

          Tell one person nearby how you feel and stay with them.

        2. 2

          Move away from anything you could use to hurt yourself.

        3. 3

          Call 14416 or 112, or go to the nearest hospital.

        4. 4

          Do not use alcohol or other substances while you feel this way.

        5. 5

          Write down one reason to stay, however small, and keep it with you.

        Never stop a prescribed mental health medicine suddenly. Stopping on your own can cause withdrawal, a return of symptoms or dangerous changes. Talk to the doctor who prescribed it before any change. Counselling and medicine often work best together.

        After childbirth: persistent sadness, guilt, scary thoughts, or feeling disconnected from your baby can be postpartum depression. It is common, treatable, and not your fault. Tell your ASHA worker or doctor — help is available.
