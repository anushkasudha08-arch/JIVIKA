Menstrual health, explained simply

Written for everyone — no medical background needed. Every section tells you when to ask for help.

A usual cycle

21–35 days between periods, bleeding for 2–7 days, and a loss of about 30–80 ml. A day or two of variation each month is normal.

Needs attention

Periods closer than 21 days or further than 35, bleeding over 7 days, or pain that stops you working every month.

Call for help

Soaking a pad every hour, fainting, fever with pelvic pain, or any bleeding after 12 months without periods.

What counts as an irregular period?

A cycle is counted from the first day of bleeding to the day before the next bleeding starts. A cycle shorter than 21 days, longer than 35 days, or varying by more than 7–9 days from month to month is usually called irregular.

Common causes: puberty and perimenopause, PCOS, thyroid problems, large weight change, high stress, very intense exercise, some medicines, and breastfeeding.

See a doctor if: you have had no period for 3 months (and are not pregnant), periods are getting shorter and lighter, bleeding lasts more than 7 days, or bleeding happens between periods or after sex.

Menstrual hygiene: the basics that prevent infection
  • Pads: change every 4–6 hours, even on light days. Wash hands before and after.
  • Cloth pads: wash with soap and water, dry fully in direct sunlight, and use a separate cloth each day. Never store a damp cloth folded.
  • Menstrual cup: empty every 8–12 hours, wash with clean water and mild soap, and boil for 5–10 minutes between cycles.
  • Tampons: change every 4–8 hours. Use the lowest absorbency needed. Do not leave one in for more than 8 hours.
  • Washing: plain water on the outside is enough. The vagina cleans itself. Do not use soap, perfume, antiseptic liquids or douching inside.
  • Disposal: wrap pads in paper and put them in a bin. Do not flush them — they block drains and pollute water.

Sudden fever with a rash, dizziness or a strong smell while using a tampon or cup needs urgent medical care.

PCOS: what it is and why early care matters

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a hormonal condition. Eggs may not release regularly, periods become irregular, and male-type hormones can rise. It affects roughly 1 in 5 women of reproductive age in India.

Common signs: periods more than 35 days apart or missing, acne, extra facial or body hair, hair thinning, weight gain around the belly, and difficulty becoming pregnant.

What helps: medical assessment (usually an ultrasound and blood tests), regular movement, steady meals with less refined sugar, good sleep, and treatment for specific concerns such as acne or fertility. There is no single "cure" — it is managed over time.

Do not: start hormonal tablets or "PCOS powders" on your own, especially without a diagnosis. Some supplements sold online can harm the liver.

Painful periods: when pain means something more

Common period pain starts a day or two before bleeding or on the first day, feels like cramping in the lower belly, and eases within 2–3 days. Heat, rest and simple pain relief usually help.

Pain that needs investigation: pain that gets worse every year, pain during sex, pain when passing stool, pain on one side, pain that continues after bleeding stops, or pain that does not respond to ordinary pain relief. Endometriosis and fibroids are common causes.

Keep a simple record of dates and pain scores — the calendar on this page is enough. A doctor can act much faster with that information.

Common myths, answered
  • "Do not bathe during periods." False. Warm water bathing reduces cramps and keeps you clean.
  • "Period blood is impure." False. It is blood and tissue from the uterus. No religious or social rule makes a woman untouchable during her period.
  • "Severe pain is normal for women." Not always. Pain that disrupts work, school or sleep deserves a medical review.
  • "You cannot get pregnant during periods." False. Sperm can survive several days, and cycle timing varies.
  • "Iron tablets are only for anaemia." Iron should follow a test and a prescription, not a guess.
When to consult a healthcare professional
  • Soaking one pad per hour for 2 hours, or passing clots larger than a rupee coin repeatedly.
  • Bleeding for more than 7 days, or bleeding between periods or after sex.
  • No period for 3 months without pregnancy, or periods suddenly stopping after years of regularity.
  • Pain so severe that you cannot stand, work or sleep.
  • Fever, foul-smelling discharge, or severe lower abdominal pain.
  • Any bleeding after 12 months without a period (after menopause).
  • Bleeding during pregnancy, severe headache or swelling in pregnancy — go the same day.
